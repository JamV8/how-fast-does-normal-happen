How fast does normal happen?

Statistics classes teach that once each sample has about 30 numbers, the averages from repeated samples follow a bell curve, the shape statisticians call normal. A lot of everyday statistics leans on that rule. For a class project I tested it with computer simulations, on textbook examples and on real data, to find out how many numbers it actually takes.

A “95% sure” range that’s right 90% of the time

Say you want the average arrival delay at New York’s airports. You pull 30 random flights from the 2013 records, average their delays, and add the usual 95% range around that average.

I ran that exact procedure 100,000 times on the real flight data. The range caught the true average only 89.8% of the time, and when it missed, it almost always missed low: in 9.8% of tries the whole range sat below the real delay. The method promises 2.5%.

Each line on the chart is one of those tries. The red ones missed.

The trouble is the shape of the data

These dots are 1,100 real arrival delays. Most flights land within 20 minutes of schedule, and more than half land early. A few are hours late. The longest delay in the data is about 21 hours, far off the right edge of this chart.

One study, one average

A study takes 30 flights and averages their delays. Most of the time it misses the rare disasters, so its average comes out a little low.

Repeat it, and the averages make their own shape

These are 2,000 studies of 30 flights each. If 30 were enough, this pile would be a symmetric bell. It still leans right, with a tail of unlucky studies that happened to include a very late flight.

How many flights is enough?

Keep scrolling to put more flights into every study. The pile tightens and evens out until it matches the bell curve (red line).

I only count it as bell-shaped when four checks pass (box on the right) and keep passing for bigger studies. For flight delays that happened at 235 flights.

Every kind of data has its own number

I ran the same test on ten kinds of data. Dice rolls settled after 8 values and the time between Old Faithful eruptions after 6. Diamond prices needed 51, flight delays 235, and county populations 2,450. The more lopsided the data, the bigger the number.

Some data never settle

A few kinds of data produce giant outliers so often that averaging doesn’t help at all. The textbook case is called the Cauchy distribution. As you scroll, each study averages more and more values, up to 5,000. The pile looks the same as it did with one.

Is 30 enough?

No.

For flight delays it takes about 235 flights before the usual 95% range behaves. With 235, my simulation caught the true average 94.1% of the time, up from 89.8% with 30.

If your data have a long tail, like the delays, prices and populations here, 30 values can produce a confident-looking range that’s wrong more often than it admits. So it’s worth plotting the data before trusting the ±.

About this project

Every number here comes from simulations I wrote in R for MATH 167R: 100,000 simulated studies for every sample size, on five computer-generated data types and five real datasets. The report has the code, the full tables, and how I chose the four checks.

  • Findings: results and the summary table
  • Real data: flights, diamonds, geysers and counties, including the 95% range test
  • Method: how “bell-shaped enough” was defined
  • Play: roll dice and race ten datasets to the bell curve
  • Lab: try any dataset and sample size yourself
  • Code on GitHub

Data

Flight delays: nycflights13 (all flights leaving New York City airports in 2013).
Diamond prices and Midwest counties: ggplot2 sample datasets.
Old Faithful eruptions and yearly inventions: datasets built into R.

Method in one line

Draw a sample, average it, repeat 100,000 times, check the shape of the averages, and find the smallest sample size where it passes. Seed 9426.
