A visual story about averages

How fast does
normal happen?

Average a group of random numbers. Do it again and again, and the averages pile up into a bell curve. Statisticians call that shape “normal”, and the rule behind it the Central Limit Theorem. Textbooks say groups of 30 are enough. I tested how many numbers it really takes.

Scroll
01 · WHY IT MATTERS

Most “± 3%” figures rely on this curve.

A news poll asks 1,000 people and reports “52%, plus or minus 3%.” That 52% is an average: every “yes” counts as 1 and every “no” as 0.

The poll is never repeated, so the bell curve fills in what would happen if it were: 95% of repeated polls would land within 3 points of the truth.

Drug trials and website experiments work the same way. If the averages are not bell-shaped yet, that ± is wrong, and nobody notices.

02 · THE DATA

Real data are rarely bell-shaped.

Each dot is one waiting time: minutes until the next customer arrives. Most waits are short, a few are very long. The shape is lopsided, nothing like a bell.

03 · ONE STUDY

A study takes a few values and averages them.

Pick five waiting times at random and report their average. That single number is all one study sees.

04 · HOW I TESTED IT

A computer can repeat the study 100,000 times.

Each dot below is one study’s average. Together they show how far a single study can land from the truth. This pile is the kind of curve behind a ±.

With five values per study it is still lopsided. I repeated this for every study size: 5, 10, 20 values and up.

05 · BIGGER STUDIES

Give every study more values.

Keep scrolling. n is the number of values each study averages. As n grows, the averages squeeze together and settle onto the bell curve (white line).

I counted it as a bell curve once four checks passed (shown at the top). With 30 waiting times, a “95%” range still misses on the high side 3.4% of the time instead of 2.5%, about 40% more often than promised. It took n = 71.

06 · WHAT I FOUND

The answer depends on the data.

Same test, ten kinds of data, from dice rolls to flight delays. Even, not-lopsided data need only a handful of values. Lopsided real-world data, like prices, delays and populations, need anywhere from about 40 to thousands.

07 · THE EXCEPTION

Some data never settle.

In some data, gigantic outliers show up so often that averaging can’t tame them. Watch n climb to 5,000: the averages stay as scattered as single values.

Real data with a few giant values come close. County populations needed 2,450. (The textbook example is called the Cauchy distribution.)

08 · THE ANSWER

So, is it thirty?

No.

It is 8 for dice, 71 for waiting times, 2,450 for county populations, and for some data no number works.

And the poll? Yes/no answers split close to 50–50 are not lopsided, so 1,000 people is plenty. That ± 3% is honest.

The lesson: before trusting a ± built on an average, check how lopsided the data are. The more lopsided, the more data you need.
