A statistics experiment · Central Limit Theorem

How fast does
normal happen?

Statistics classes teach a rule of thumb: once you average 30 or more observations, the average follows a bell curve. That bell curve is what makes margins of error and confidence intervals work. I put the rule to the test with millions of simulated studies, on textbook distributions and on real data.

The idea in three pictures

Averages forget where they came from

Take any population, even a lopsided one. Pick a few values at random and average them. Repeat that thousands of times. The averages pile up into a bell curve. That is the Central Limit Theorem. The only question is how many values each average needs.

Why anyone should care
52% ± 3.1%

Every margin of error relies on this theorem

A pollster asks 1,000 people one question and reports “52%, plus or minus 3.1 points.” They never repeat the poll; the bell curve tells them how far off a single poll could be. The same logic is behind drug trials, A/B tests on websites, and quality control in factories.

If the averages have not become a bell curve yet, those margins of error are wrong, and nobody notices, because in real life you only see one sample.

The answer

So, is 30 enough?

Sometimes 30 is more than enough. Often it is not nearly enough. And sometimes no number is enough.

What decides the answer

Three things slow the bell curve down

↗

Lopsidedness

The longer one tail, the more values each average needs. One simple formula predicted the answer for most populations, textbook and real:

n ≈ (skewness ÷ 0.25)²
▥

Lumpiness

Counts like 0, 1, 2 move in steps. Even when they are not very lopsided, the steps keep the shape rough for longer. Rare-event counts needed values, more than the waiting times.

⚡

Extreme values

If giant values are common enough, averaging never tames them. For the Cauchy distribution no sample size works: averaging 5,000 values is no better than looking at one.

Real data, real consequences

What this means for actual data

To trust an ordinary 95% interval for the average of…

…flight delays at NYC airports
flights

Most flights are on time, but a few are hours late. With 30 flights the “95%” interval misses on the high side of the time instead of 2.5%.

…diamond prices
diamonds

A few very expensive stones stretch the price distribution, so 30 diamonds are not quite enough.

…county populations in the US Midwest
counties

A handful of giant counties (Chicago’s Cook County has 5 million people; the typical county has 35,000) make the average jump around. That is more than five times the 437 counties these five states actually have.

…waiting times at Old Faithful geyser
eruptions

The waits come in two humps, short and long, but are nearly symmetric. Here the rule of 30 is more than enough.

How I judged “normal enough”

Four checks, one standard for everyone

There is no official cutoff, so I defined one and applied it to every population. The averages count as normal enough only when all four checks pass.

Not lopsided

Skewness between −0.25 and 0.25.

No excess extremes

Excess kurtosis between −0.5 and 0.5.

Straight Q-Q plot

Correlation of at least 0.998 with a perfect bell curve.

Honest 95% intervals

On each side, 1.75%–3.25% of averages fall outside (the ideal is 2.5%).

Each threshold was calibrated by simulation: see how and why. I also report every result under a stricter and a more lenient standard.
