source("../R/clt_functions.R")

rgen  <- function(n) rnorm(n, mean = 0, sd = 1)
mu    <- 0
sigma <- 1

coarse <- c(1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50, 100)
exper  <- run_with_refinement(rgen, coarse, mu, sigma)

res    <- exper$results
n_main <- smallest_normal_n(res)
n_std  <- n_by_standard(res)
at     <- function(k) res[res$n == k, ]

save_results(res, "normal")
save_summary(res, "Normal(0,1)", n_main, prediction = "1",
             key_note = "Control case: normal at every n")

1. What population am I sampling from?

The standard normal distribution: the bell curve itself. It is symmetric, has no skewness, and its tails are exactly as heavy as the normal benchmark. It is the control case of the study: if the simulation and the diagnostics work correctly, the sample means should pass every check at every sample size.

5. When did the sampling distribution become approximately normal?

\[\boxed{n = 1}\]

6. Why did I choose that value?

  • At n = 1 the skewness is -0.008, the excess kurtosis -0.014, the Q-Q correlation 1.0000, and the tails are 2.55% / 2.48%, all essentially perfect. The same holds at every larger n.
  • Even the Shapiro-Wilk test, which I do not use for decisions because it is very sensitive, rejected normality at the 5% level for only 0 of the 9 sample sizes. That is about what chance alone would produce for truly normal data.
  • This confirms the tools: on data that really are normal, my simulation and my four checks do not create false alarms. That makes the failures in the other populations meaningful.

7. What surprised me?

This one was not a surprise. The sample means were normal already at n = 1 under all three standards, which makes sense because averaging normal values always gives a normal result. It was also a useful check that my simulation code was not distorting anything.

## 6. Why did I choose that value?

- At n = 1 the skewness is `r f3(at(1)$skewness)`, the excess kurtosis `r f3(at(1)$ex_kurtosis)`,
  the Q-Q correlation `r f4(at(1)$qq_cor)`, and the tails are `r pct(at(1)$lower_tail, 2)` /
  `r pct(at(1)$upper_tail, 2)`, all essentially perfect. The same holds at every larger n.
- Even the Shapiro-Wilk test, which I do not use for decisions because it is very sensitive,
  rejected normality at the 5% level for only `r sum(res$shapiro_p < 0.05)` of the
  `r nrow(res)` sample sizes. That is about what chance alone would produce for truly normal data.
- This confirms the tools: on data that really are normal, my simulation and my four checks
  do not create false alarms. That makes the failures in the other populations meaningful.
