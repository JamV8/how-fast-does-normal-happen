1. What population am I sampling from?

A fair six-sided die: the values 1 to 6, each with probability 1/6. It is the opposite of a bell curve. It is flat (no peak), bounded (no extreme values at all), and discrete (only six possible values). It is, however, perfectly symmetric.

Standard deviation σ √(35/12) ≈ 1.708

Excess kurtosis −1.27 (light tails: flat, no extremes)
Theoretical SE of the mean 1.708/√n

n = 15. The die is symmetric (skewness 0) but flat and discrete. In my pilot run it clearly failed at n = 1 and passed every check at n = 15, so I predicted 15.

  1. Coarse grid: n = 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 32, 34, 36, 38, 40, 45, 50, 60, 75, 100, 200. It is denser between 30 and 50 to locate the strict standard.

5. When did the sampling distribution become approximately normal?

\[\boxed{n = 8}\]

6. Why did I choose that value?

  • Skewness never mattered. It was -0.009 at n = 1 and stayed near zero, because the die is symmetric.
  • The flat shape faded quickly. Excess kurtosis follows −1.27/n and passed my ±0.5 limit at n = 3.
  • The Q-Q correlation was the last check to pass, at n = 8. At small n the Q-Q plot is a staircase with an S-shape: the steps come from discreteness, and the S comes from the missing tails (a sum of a few dice can never be very far from 3.5).
  • At n = 8 all four checks pass: skewness -0.014, kurtosis -0.167, Q-Q r 0.9980, tails 2.44% / 2.30%. Every larger n also passes. Every tested sample size below this n fails at least one check.

7. What surprised me?

I expected the die to stay visibly discrete for a long time, since it only has six values. It passed my main standard at n = 8. What surprised me more is that it also passed the strict standard at around n = 38. Its skewness was zero from the start, so the only problems were the flat shape and the gaps between possible values. With n rolls the mean can take 5n + 1 different values, and by n = 40 the gaps are less than a tenth of a standard error. So even for a discrete distribution like a die, a larger n improves the approximation quite well.

## 1. What population am I sampling from?

A fair six-sided die: the values 1 to 6, each with probability 1/6. It is the opposite of a bell curve.
It is **flat** (no peak), **bounded** (no extreme values at all), and **discrete** (only six possible
values). It is, however, perfectly **symmetric**.

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Mean μ | 3.5 |
| Standard deviation σ | √(35/12) ≈ 1.708 |
| Skewness | 0 (symmetric) |
| Excess kurtosis | −1.27 (light tails: flat, no extremes) |
| Theoretical SE of the mean | 1.708/√n |

## 6. Why did I choose that value?

- **Skewness never mattered.** It was `r f3(at(1)$skewness)` at n = 1 and stayed near zero, because the
  die is symmetric.
- **The flat shape faded quickly.** Excess kurtosis follows −1.27/n and passed my ±0.5 limit at
  n = `r first_stable_n(res$n, res$kurt_ok)`.
- **The Q-Q correlation was the last check to pass**, at n = `r first_stable_n(res$n, res$qq_ok)`.
  At small n the Q-Q plot is a staircase with an S-shape: the steps come from discreteness, and the S
  comes from the missing tails (a sum of a few dice can never be very far from 3.5).
- At **n = `r n_main`** all four checks pass: skewness `r f3(at(n_main)$skewness)`, kurtosis
  `r f3(at(n_main)$ex_kurtosis)`, Q-Q r `r f4(at(n_main)$qq_cor)`, tails
  `r pct(at(n_main)$lower_tail, 2)` / `r pct(at(n_main)$upper_tail, 2)`. Every larger n also passes.
  `r flip_text(res, n_main)`
